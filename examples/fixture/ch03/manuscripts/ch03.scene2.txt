Mu-jin was in the yard behind the hall, sitting on a crate with his left leg stretched out straight in front of him, the way a man sits when the leg is fine and he has been thinking about it anyway.

“Eighteen percent,” he said, without turning. “Still eighteen. She didn't ask me to take it back.”

“She asked you about me.”

“She asked me everything about you. Where you were from, who trained you, what you ate.” He shifted the leg. “I told her the truth, which is that I don't know any of it, and that you were eleven minutes early.”

Do-yoon sat down on the next crate. Four hunters crossed the yard toward the gate lists. None of them looked at a pair of porters.

“Mister Park. The gate's suspended.”

“I can read.”

“The reinspection was because of the cores. The cores were high because we took the right corridor.” He said it carefully, laying it down the way you lay something down in front of a man who is allowed to pick it up or not. “If we had gone left, the ceiling comes down, the third chamber empties into the first, and nobody ever counts what was in it. The gate stays a D-gate on paper for months.”

Mu-jin was quiet long enough for someone across the yard to shout a name that was not theirs.

“You're telling me the gate got flagged because I lived.”

“I'm telling you the gate got flagged because the third chamber stayed dry.”

“That's the same sentence with the blood wiped off it.” He pulled out the cigarette he had not lit inside the gate yesterday and turned it over between two fingers. “Say the rest.”

“There is no rest.”

“Kid.” Mu-jin finally looked at him. “Yesterday you knew about a room in a gate you had never been inside. Today you know why a reinspection happened before the survey division does. I'm old. I'm not stupid. And I'm not asking.” He put the cigarette away. “I'm telling you that whatever it is, it moves things. You should have worked that out before you opened your mouth in that corridor.”

“I did work it out.”

“And?”

“I'd rather have you than the gate.”

Mu-jin made a sound that was not a word, and looked out at the yard for a while.

“Eighteen percent,” he said again. “Association's going to run that survey Thursday. They'll want a porter who's been inside. That's you now, and that's not a favor.”

Do-yoon thought about Thursday, and found nothing there. Nothing at all: no shape, no rumor, no remembered headline. The first time, Thursday had been a day he spent carrying water filters in Guro.

He had eleven days of future left that he trusted, and he had just spent one of them on an old man's leg.

Across the yard, Yoon Ha-eun stood at the hall door with her tablet against her hip, watching the two of them the way a surveyor watches a ceiling.
